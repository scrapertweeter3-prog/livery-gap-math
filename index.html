The policy in the glovebox doesn't know about the app

A personal auto policy was priced for a commuter, and it asks one question when a claim comes in: were the wheels working a job? Delivery miles are livery use, and livery is written out of the standard policy before the first premium is ever quoted. The adjuster's second question, were you delivering at the time, turns a fender claim into a walk-away. The card in the glovebox covers the commute. It has never heard of the app.

The fix exists and it prices like pocket change. A delivery endorsement runs about $24 a month. Three shifts a week is twelve a month, so the rider works out to $2 a shift, 50 cents an hour on a four-hour shift, 2.8 percent of a $72 gross. It is the cheapest line on the shift's ledger and the only one that decides whether the shift's worst day is survivable.

The gap lives in the waiting lane. The app's own policy covers the loaded trip. The miles spent app-on and offer-hunting belong to the personal policy, and the personal policy says no. A rear-end at a light while the screen is open becomes $9,000 of car that nobody pays for, with the other driver's $30,000 hospital bill still landing in the mailbox. The coverage was never purchased; the button on the dash just made that obvious.

The break-even is absurd in the driver's favor. The endorsement is $288 a year. One saved claim on a $9,000 car pays for 31 years of riders. On the liability side, a single $30,000 walk-away equals 104 years of premiums. No other $2 line on the shift carries three decades of tail risk, and none of the other lines can be revoked retroactively by a question on a phone call.

The ledger: $24 a month, 12 shifts, $2 a shift. 50 cents an hour on a four-hour shift, 2.8 percent of a $72 gross. $288 a year. $9,000 car, one claim in 31 years breaks even. $30,000 claim, 104 years. The payout screen prices the drops, the endorsement prices the miles nobody else will insure, and only one of those prices is optional at quote time.
